Introduction

From basic Chemistry, we know that atoms bond with each other to form molecules, but have you ever wondered what the shapes of these molecules are? Can’t atoms just bond in infinitely many different orientations?

To answer this question, we use VSEPR Theory.

VSEPR Theory, or Valence Shell Electron-Pair Repulsion Theory, allows us to predict the three-dimensional structure of molecules by examining the number of bonds and lone electron pairs in their Lewis structures.

Core Idea of VSEPR Theory

Electrons repel each other.

Because electrons carry the same negative charge, regions of high electron density around a central atom push away from one another. To minimize this repulsion, these regions arrange themselves as far apart as possible in three-dimensional space. Electrons in the valence shell may exist as:

  • Bonding pairs – electrons shared between atoms
  • Lone pairs – electrons localized on the central atom

Regions of Electron Density

Each of the following counts as one region:

  • Single, double, or triple bond
  • Lone pair

Electron Domain Geometry (EDG)

Electron Domain Geometry (EDG) describes the spatial arrangement of all regions of electron density around the central atom. This includes both bonding pairs and lone pairs.

EDG depends only on the total number of electron regions, not on whether those regions are bonds or lone pairs. Different molecules can have the same EDG but different shapes.

For example, a molecule with four regions of electron density will always have a tetrahedral EDG, regardless of how many of those regions are lone pairs.

Molecular Geometry (MG)

Molecular Geometry (MG) describes the actual shape of a molecule based only on the positions of the atoms, not the lone pairs.

Lone pairs occupy space and repel bonding pairs more strongly than bonding pairs repel each other. This causes bond angles to shrink and changes the observed shape of the molecule.

For example:

  • NH₃ has a tetrahedral EDG but a trigonal pyramidal MG
  • H₂O has a tetrahedral EDG but a bent MG

AXE Notation

AXE notation is a shorthand system used in VSEPR theory to describe molecular structure.

  • A represents the central atom
  • X represents the number of atoms bonded to the central atom
  • E represents the number of lone pairs on the central atom

The values of X and E determine both the electron domain geometry and the molecular geometry of a molecule.

Connecting AXE, EDG, and MG

VSEPR analysis follows a clear sequence:

  1. Determine the AXE notation from the Lewis structure
  2. Use the total number of electron regions (X + E) to find the EDG
  3. Ignore lone pairs to determine the molecular geometry

For example, an AX₃E₁ molecule has four electron regions, giving a tetrahedral EDG. Since one region is a lone pair, the molecular geometry becomes trigonal pyramidal.

Common AXE Examples

  • AX₂ → Linear EDG, Linear MG (CO₂)
  • AX₃ → Trigonal planar EDG, Trigonal planar MG (BF₃)
  • AX₄ → Tetrahedral EDG, Tetrahedral MG (CH₄)
  • AX₃E → Tetrahedral EDG, Trigonal pyramidal MG (NH₃)
  • AX₂E₂ → Tetrahedral EDG, Bent MG (H₂O)
